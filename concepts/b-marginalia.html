Software That Has To Hold

On proctoring clients that cannot crash, machine learning that has to be checkable, and why almost all of the interesting work happens below the part anyone demos.

An essay by Sribhuvan Yellu. Evidence in the margin.

The first thing I learned shipping exam software is that the demo is never the product. A proctoring client looks trivial when you show it: a window opens, a camera turns on, a candidate takes a test. The actual product is everything that happens when a laptop sleeps mid-exam, when a webcam driver stops answering, when someone alt-tabs at second four hundred of a three hour certification. Those are not edge cases. At the volumes exam delivery runs at, they are Tuesday.

I work on the OnVUE proctoring client at Pearson.1 Right now I am moving it onto a shared-UI codebase so that Windows and macOS ship from one source tree instead of two that drift apart quietly for a year. That sentence is boring and the work is not. Two platforms that drifted apart means two different answers to what happens when the camera goes away, and only one of them was ever tested properly.

Most of my time goes into the bridges between native platform code and the web layer, carrying camera and microphone through so BrowserLock keeps its grip on the session. A bridge is where the honest bugs live. Above it, the web app assumes a stream exists. Below it, the operating system reserves the right to take that stream away for reasons it will not explain. Somebody has to write down what happens in between, and if nobody does, the answer becomes whatever the last person to touch it happened to believe.

Trust is a device problem

The usual instinct in proctoring is to watch the human harder. More video, more flags, more reviewers. I think that instinct is mostly wrong, or at least badly sequenced. Before you have any business judging a person's behavior, you have to know the machine is what it claims to be. Otherwise you are doing expensive inference on a feed you cannot vouch for.

That belief came out of building the Android Chromebook exam client.2 It is Kotlin, it uses Lock Task Mode to pin the device to one app for the duration of a test, and it carries camera and mic over a WebSocket bridge between the native shell and the exam web app. The piece I would defend hardest is the least visible one: Play Integrity attestation, so the backend can verify the device before it releases a single exam question. Nothing about that shows up in a screenshot. It is the difference between a secure exam and a picture of one.

Integrity checks belong at the start of the session, not in the review queue afterward. Everything downstream inherits whatever you failed to establish up front.

Lock Task Mode also taught me something about scope. You get a device that will not let the user leave, which sounds like total control until you remember that you now own every failure. There is no browser to fall back to and no home screen to escape into. If your client wedges, a person in a testing room has lost their afternoon and their fee. That shapes how you write code. You stop reaching for clever and start reaching for recoverable.

The model is the cheap part

Before the client work I spent a year putting models against live exam video.3 Gemini 2.5 Flash on the feed, thirty frames a second, ninety-two percent detection on flagged behaviors. That number is the one people ask about and it is the least interesting thing I built that year.

3. Pearson VUE 2024 – 2025 · Applied AI Engineer, intern
  • Gemini 2.5 Flash on live exam video, 92% detection on flagged behaviors at 30 FPS.
  • Cache-augmented generation so repeat policy lookups hit stored context, not the model.
  • MCP server and LangGraph assistant giving an LLM real read and write access to scheduling tools.

The interesting part was the plumbing around it. Policy lookups repeat constantly in a system like this, and paying a model to reread the same policy document a thousand times a day is a decision, not a law of nature. So the pipeline uses cache-augmented generation: repeat lookups hit stored context instead of the model. That one change moves the cost curve more than any prompt I have ever written.

The other piece was an MCP server and a LangGraph assistant that let a model actually read and write real scheduling tools rather than describe what it would do if it could. This is where I think a lot of applied AI work goes soft. A demo assistant produces convincing text. A useful one takes an action that a human can later audit and reverse. Those are different engineering problems and only the second one has consequences.

I hold the same line on my own tools. I run a multi-agent Claude workflow for review and refactor passes, which sounds like a productivity claim and is really a discipline claim. Agents are good at surfacing what I stopped noticing in code I wrote last month. They are not good at deciding what the code should be. I keep that boundary sharp because the moment it blurs, you are shipping things nobody read.

Research code deserves better

My complaint about research code is not that it is messy. It is that the reasoning evaporates. Somebody chooses a normalization, a threshold, a train and test split, and six months later the choice survives in a script while the argument for it does not survive anywhere. The result is work that runs and cannot be defended.

Expression Explorer4 is my answer to that. It does gene expression analysis: group comparison, signature scoring, survival analysis. Two decisions in it matter more than the feature list. New disease areas plug in through a shared contract instead of a fork, because forking per disease is how a codebase becomes five codebases that disagree. And every analytical decision is written down where a reader can find it rather than buried in a function body. If a biologist cannot tell what the pipeline assumed, the pipeline has not finished its job.

The research I published sits on the same premise. The EMBC paper5 was about turning spatial representations into adaptive insight for endoscopic polyp diagnosis, and my thesis work6 put domain-expert structure into graph neural networks for methane prediction in anaerobic digestion. The reason to guide a network with expert structure is not accuracy alone. It is that an engineer who already understands digestion can look at the graph and say where it is wrong.

What I build when nobody is asking

The side projects are not a portfolio. They are where I test opinions I cannot test at work. AutoQuanta7 trains and compares models entirely on your own machine because I do not accept that every experiment needs to leave the building. Code Agent8 runs its own edits inside a Docker sandbox and traces every step in LangSmith, on the theory that an agent you cannot replay is an agent you cannot trust. RoadEye9 does road damage detection on device, and most of the real work there was deduplicating the same pothole across frames, geotagging it, and estimating its actual size. CipherCloud10 is zero-knowledge encrypted storage, which is mainly an exercise in refusing to hold the key.

There is a pattern and I did not plan it. Each one moves a decision closer to the person affected by it. Training on your own hardware, replaying your own agent, encrypting so the host cannot read you. I did not set out to have a politics about software. Building this much of it gave me one.

What ties it together is a preference I will state plainly. I would rather understand one system all the way down than touch twenty at the surface. Depth is not a personality trait here, it is a bet about where the failures are. The failures are never in the part you can see on a slide. They are in the bridge, in the attestation, in the assumption nobody wrote down. That is the work I want.
